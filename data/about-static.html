About Tongues from Space

Using the site

Why Tongues from Space

A glacier ends in its tongue: the lowest stretch of ice, where the glacier is thinnest and melts the most. It is also where change is easiest to see. Every summer the tip pulls back and leaves bare rock, a lake or a new valley floor behind, and a satellite pixel of 10 m is fine enough to show it.

I believe in pictures. A number can be read a hundred times without it staying with you, but a before-and-after image is understood at once and remembered. That is why the pictures come first here, and the figures sit next to them.

The pictures are real satellite images of the day shown, not renderings. The one exception, the optional Super-resolution layer in 3D, is labelled as such.

I hope this site sparks awareness of glaciers and of why they matter to society, for instance because of the water they store. That is why the ice is also shown in litres, swimming pools and Lake Zurich. — Jochem Braakhekke

How to use it

Left panel: three steps

  • 1 Choose a glacier: one of the ten largest Swiss glaciers, from the list or by clicking it on the map.
  • 2 Compare: the view is Map or 3D (the camera flies between the two; switch "Fly between map and 3D" off to change at once). The mode is One year, Slider (drag the line to compare two years) or Timelapse (one picture per year, with a speed and an optional loop). The colour composition has four choices (see The colours). "Adjust image" changes the brightness and the opacity of the image and of its surroundings, and the Metadata button (under Save and share) names the bands and the pixel size of the picture.
  • 3 Create and share is described under Create and share.

Right panel: three tabs

  • Discover holds cards with what to look for, news, a fun fact, the origin of the name, a hike and a bike route near the tongue, and links to the scenes in the Copernicus Browser.
  • Stats holds the cards Area, Length, Thickness and volume, Ice speed and Water storage. Their tiles follow the years you select.
  • Switzerland puts the glacier in perspective and turns the ice lost over the selected years into water.

A card opens with a click and the one before it closes. The small numbers jump to the list of Sources; a dash or a note says when a figure does not exist for a glacier.

On a phone

The map comes first, then the left panel, then the story. Two fingers move the map. The map can be opened in the 3D view from the same switch.

Create and share

Image

The view as it is shown, map or 3D, as one JPEG picture.

Animation

A short MP4 video made in your browser from the slider, the timelapse or the 3D view. You choose:

  • the format: 16:9 landscape, 9:16 portrait or 4:5 for the Instagram feed;
  • the framing: the tongue, the whole glacier or the whole image box;
  • a fact card at the end: Glacier facts, Ice, Retreat or Size.

The figures on the cards of pictures and videos follow the same rules as the right panel: the ice and water figures are the estimates described under Ice and water, and the outline, length and area figures are GLAMOS values.

Share, elevation model, gallery, size

  • Share opens the share sheet of your device, or copies the link. The link opens this glacier with the same mode, years and colour composition.
  • Elevation model (3D view, glaciers with yearly surfaces) downloads the ice surface of a year as GeoTIFF, ESRI ASCII grid or XYZ points, in LV95 (EPSG:2056). It is a model, not a measurement, and for non-commercial use.
  • Gallery (button under the title) shows the pictures and videos that visitors have shared. When you send one you confirm that you made it and agree that it is shown. It is checked and waits for approval before it appears.
  • Size visualizer (above the tabs in the right panel) opens a page where you see the ice of the glacier as a block next to your own city (Volume 3D), or drag its outline onto the city (Area 2D).
Size visualizer

A separate page, the Size visualizer, answers one question: how big is a glacier? It is opened from the button above the tabs in the right panel, and it works for the ten glaciers of this site.

  • Volume (3D): the ice of the glacier as a block, or as a ball, that you can drop onto the terrain next to a city or a landmark. The volumes are the estimates described under Ice and water.

  • Pick a city, or type the name of one of your own. In 3D there are landmarks too.

The pictures

The satellite images

Choosing the scene

One real Sentinel-2 scene per glacier and year: the clearest view between 1 July and 20 September. Every acquisition is scored for cloud and snow cover; the best September scene wins, otherwise August, otherwise July.

The pictures are rendered from Level-1C data with the Copernicus Browser true colour process, which keeps shaded slopes natural.

The picking tool

For some glaciers the scene of each year was chosen with a separate picking tool instead (scripts/tools/pick-scenes.html). It scores every acquisition from 1 August to the middle of October for cloud, cloud shadow, mountain shadow, snow and haze, in the bottom 3 km of the tongue and over the whole image. It prefers days near 30 September and keeps one satellite orbit for all years.

  • No cloud, cloud shadow or big mountain shadow is allowed in the bottom 3 km (the limits are loosened only for a year that would otherwise have no scene).
  • Not more than 10 % cloud anywhere in the image.

Why not after the 20th

This is for the automatic choice. Swiss glacier monitoring counts in glaciological years (1 October to 30 September) and GLAMOS measures at the end of the melt season, so September images match the annual figures: the picture of summer 2017 shows the ice at the end of the glaciological year 2017 (see Years and periods).

After about 20 September fresh snow often hides the tongue; in 2024 the limit is 14 September, because heavy snow fell on the 15th.

Before 2016: Landsat

Sentinel-2 starts in 2016. A switch under the years adds real Landsat 5 and 7 pictures for the years from 1985 to 2013 (Collection 2 Level-2, USGS). Their pixels are 30 m instead of Sentinel-2's 10 m, so they show less detail.

  • One scene per year, by the same rules: 1 July to 20 September, the clear scene closest to 1 September, the whole picture covered where there is such a scene. Years without a usable scene have no picture.
  • All three colour styles work: natural colour (red, green, blue) and the false colour styles from the near and shortwave infrared bands that Landsat has too (false colour 1: near infrared, red, green; false colour 2: near infrared and the two shortwave infrared bands, the counterparts of Sentinel-2 B8A, B11 and B12).
  • The colours of the years are matched to each other on a patch of ground without ice, so that sensors and seasons look alike. Every picture is shifted onto the Sentinel-2 mosaic where it lies off.
  • Landsat 7 pictures after 2003 have stripes of missing data. I fill them by interpolating along the direction of the ice flow, read from the streaks in the picture itself (medial moraines, flow lines). That is a repair of the picture, not data.
  • Landsat 8 (2013 to 2015) is not used: its surface reflectance is not usable over ice, shade and water.
  • The pictures keep the real 30 m pixels of Landsat (one picture pixel = one Landsat pixel, cut with nearest neighbour) and are shown without smoothing, so no detail is invented. They are not corrected for slope shading.
  • In the 3D view they are draped over the ice surface of their year where the glacier has a terrain file with a surface for each year (before 2011 over the surface of 2011), otherwise over the one elevation model.
  • For a glacier so small that its picture is under 400 Landsat pixels across, the switch says "not recommended".
Sentinel-2

Two European satellites of the Copernicus programme (2A since 2015, 2B since 2017), looking straight down on a strip 290 km wide.

Spatial: pixels of 10 m (blue, green, red and near infrared), 20 m (six bands) or 60 m (three). Details under about 10 m are invisible. Natural and False 1 use 10 m bands; False 2 uses 20 m bands and looks softer; False 3 uses one 20 m band (B11).

Spectral: 13 bands, from visible light (about 443 nm) to shortwave infrared (about 2190 nm).

Temporal: each satellite returns every 10 days, both together every 5 days at the equator and more often at higher latitudes. Over Aletsch I counted about 3 acquisitions a week and up to 4 (32, 33 and 41 days between 1 July and 20 September in 2023 to 2025). Clouds, shadow and fresh snow remove many of them.

The colours

Each picture combines three bands as red, green and blue. The four buttons in step 2 carry nicknames: Natural, Infra (False 1), Bloody (False 2) and Hot? (False 3). The false colours are a code, not what you would see.

Natural (B04, B03, B02): as the eye sees it. Snow white, rock grey, plants green.

False 1 (B08, B04, B03), infrared: near infrared shown as red. Plants red, snow white, bare ice grey, water dark/blue; this sets bare ice, snow and vegetation apart.

False 2 (B8A, B11, B12): snow and ice red, clouds white, rock pale, water black. Experimental, handy to tell ice from cloud.

False 3 (B04, B11, B02, as a ratio picture): ice and snow yellow to orange, rock blue. Red is the red/SWIR ratio, green the blue band, blue the SWIR (shortwave infrared).

Why only these ten glaciers

The Swiss Glacier Inventory SGI2023 lists 1,289 glaciers with a total area of 860 km². The ten largest hold 28 % of it; 1,048 are smaller than 0.5 km² and 673 smaller than 0.1 km².

With 10 m pixels a glacier of 0.1 km² is only about 1,000 pixels: its tongue is a few pixels wide, and a retreat of a few metres a year is less than one pixel. The ten largest have tongues many pixels wide that retreat by tens of metres, which shows clearly.

The numbers

Years and periods

Swiss glacier monitoring counts in glaciological years, from 1 October to 30 September. A yearly value is named after the year in which it ends: 2017 is October 2016 to September 2017, and the year ending in September 2026 is the 2026 value (the 2025/2026 measurement period).

That is the melt season whose end the satellite picture of summer 2017 shows, so a picture and the value of its year belong together. Each bar in the charts is one such year.

Selecting years

Years you select in step 2 count with both ends. A sum over 2017–2019 adds the values of 2017, 2018 and 2019, which is the change from autumn 2016 to autumn 2019. One picture of 2017 gives the values of 2017 alone.

The charts always show all years and mark the chosen ones with a red line under their label. The sums are given under the chart and in the tiles. Ice volumes (the Swiss ice, the ice of a glacier) are stocks at the end of a year, so they are read at the end of September.

Estimates

Everything derived from yearly values for a chosen period (thickness, volume and balance of a glacier, the Swiss loss, the water that melted) is an estimate from the yearly mass balance of GLAMOS, calibrated on the measured volume surveys. It is not a new measurement of the period.

Glacier figures

Area

All figures are from GLAMOS; a dash means no value. Area: Swiss Glacier Inventories SGI1850 and SGI2023. When tributaries separate, part of an area drop is a change of definition, not only melt. The area card compares the glacier in the inventories of 1850, 1973 and the newest one.

Length

Length change is measured at the tongue; negative means retreat. GLAMOS publishes only the change between surveys, never the length, so the lengths are derived.

  • The latest length is read from a GLAMOS overview figure, which prints one length per glacier. The year shown with it is where the last bar of that glacier ends in the figure (2025 for most, 2023 for Corbassière and Zmutt, which are surveyed every few years).
  • "Length in 1870" (or the year of the first survey) is that length plus all the retreat recorded since then: for Aletsch 22.11 km + 3.387 km = about 25.5 km.
  • That is exact only when the surveys follow each other without gaps and reach the year of the figure. Otherwise the tile shows "≈" and the real value may differ by the retreat of the missing years.

The Change tile and the yearly bars

  • Change adds up the length surveys that end in the chosen years. The survey that starts in the autumn before the first chosen year counts, as it ends in that year.
  • It says when the surveys cover a shorter period, because one survey can span several years and the newest data end before the newest images.
  • Retreat per year shows every year from 2016 on and marks the chosen ones.
  • Where GLAMOS measured the length only over several years (for example 2017 to 2020 at Corbassière), the change is spread evenly over those years and the bars are dashed. Years without any survey stay empty.
Thickness and volume

Volume change from surveys

Mean ice thickness change, volume change and mass balance come from the GLAMOS volume-change survey of this glacier: aerial surveys give elevation models of the glacier surface, and the difference between two of them shows how much ice was lost in between.

  • The newest survey that starts closest to 2016 sets the size of the loss for this glacier (the headline figure and the survey period). It is not your selected years.
  • Mass balance is the volume change turned into metres of water per year with a density of 850 kg/m³; the ± is the uncertainty GLAMOS gives.
  • Only surveys that cover at least 90 % of the glacier are used.

The three tiles follow the years you select. They add up (volume, and thickness, which is the volume over the mean area of the survey) or average (balance, as GLAMOS measured it, not scaled) the yearly mass balance of those years, scaled to the volume survey of the glacier, so they are estimates. They show the survey period only when no yearly value exists for the selected years.

Radar ice thickness

Single measurements from ground-penetrating radar, taken from the surface along lines, between 1958 and 2017 depending on the glacier. The map shows the mean of every 50 m cell. They tell how thick the ice was when it was measured; today it is thinner.

Thickest ice is one number with its year and its source. It is the maximum of the ice thickness map of Grab et al.11 for this glacier, in the reference year of that map (2017 for Aletsch), and the pin on the map marks the cell where it is. The card also gives the uncertainty of the map in that cell, towards thicker (+) and thinner (−) ice, as the data collection states it. It is a model result and not a measurement: the radar lines are used to calibrate an ice-flow model that fills the gaps. Only a radar or seismic maximum measured after the reference year of the map would replace it, and then the card and the pin say so.

The map layer "Ice thickness 2016 (modelled)" shows the whole map. The radar data are only a part of what is needed for the glacier ice volume of Grab et al.11, which also uses models.

Ice thickness change per year

The chart shows the yearly mass balance from GLAMOS for the years of the images, as metres of ice (the balance in water divided by 0.85). Aletsch, Rhone, Corbassière and Findel are measured directly every year; for the other six glaciers the line of all Swiss glaciers is shown.

Switch between Per year and Cumulative (the bars added up from autumn 2015). The years you select in step 2 are marked and added up under the chart.

A mass balance is not the same measurement as a change of the surface height, but over decades the two add up to nearly the same. The headline above the tiles comes from the elevation-model surveys, which cover several years each.

Ice speed

Where it comes from

The speed of the ice (layer Ice speed and the card Ice speed in Stats) comes from the ITS_LIVE annual mosaics of image pair velocities, version 2.1 (120 m, one mosaic per RGI region, 2013 to 2024).4 The product follows features of the ice surface between pairs of satellite images (Landsat 8/9, Sentinel-2 and Sentinel-1).

The values are copied from the product and only cut to the picture box of the glacier. Under 5 m/yr the product cannot tell ice from rock (rock beside the glaciers reads 1 to 2 m/yr), so nothing is drawn there, and only the ice inside the oldest outline is shown.

On the map

  • The layer shows the year of the picture (with the slider the right-hand year, in a timelapse the frame). Years before the first or after the last in the file have no speed map, and the note says so.
  • A pin marks the fastest ice of the year: the highest value along a line from the tongue up the glacier, after a running median over about 400 m so that one bad cell cannot set it. Other branches of the glacier can be faster.
  • A click on the glacier, with the layer on, shows the speed of that place in every year (the mean of the 3 × 3 cells around the click).
  • The card gives the value of the pin per year. The pin, the click values and the card are computed here from the product and say so.
  • Speed is not shown in 3D or in the downloads.
Ice and water

Ice in Switzerland

Swiss glaciers held about 45.1 km³ of ice at the end of 2025 (GLAMOS, Swiss-wide series, which gives the volume of every year since 1915). The explorer uses the years from 2016. 1 km³ of ice gives about 0.9 trillion litres of water.

Ice of one glacier

Grab et al.11 modelled the ice around 2016 (the map gives each glacier its own reference year, 2017 for Aletsch). From there the explorer uses what GLAMOS measured for this glacier:

  • its volume survey (how much ice it lost between two elevation models) says how much ice one metre of yearly mass balance is worth for it;
  • the yearly balance (this glacier's own where it is measured, else the balance of all Swiss glaciers) spreads the loss over the years;
  • for the newest autumn, which has no yearly balance yet, the Swiss loss of that year is turned into a balance (with the same ratio of ice volume to balance as in the survey years) and spread over this glacier in the same way.

Big glaciers lose a smaller share than the Swiss average, which is dominated by small glaciers, so this gives more ice than a plain national percentage. That is a best estimate and not a measurement.

Water and comparisons

The Switzerland tab turns the ice lost over your chosen years (from the end of the year before the first one to the end of the last one) into litres and four comparisons: Olympic swimming pools, Lake Zurich, the yearly Swiss tap water and the household water use per resident (9 million residents, 142 litres a day, SVGW). The text chips on the cards use the same sizes. The sizes:

  • Eiffel Tower: 330 m high (with antennas, toureiffel.paris)
  • Football pitch: 105 × 68 m = 7,140 m² (usual international size)
  • City bus: about 12 m long (standard bus, an articulated one is 18 m)
  • Olympic swimming pool: 50 × 25 × 2 m = 2.5 million litres
  • Lake Zurich: 3.9 km³
  • Swiss water supply: 931 million m³ in 2022 (SVGW)

The 3D view

A strip along the glacier, from 1 km below the tongue tip up to about 6.5 km up the glacier and 4 km wide, with the real satellite picture of the chosen year draped over the terrain. The heights are the newest edition of swisstopo's swissALTI3D elevation model (served as tiles by Mapterhorn and stored with the page on a 14 m grid).14

Drag to turn all the way round and tilt, Shift-drag or right-drag (two fingers on a phone) to move, scroll or pinch to zoom. The layers menu, the measuring tools and the downloads work here as on the map.

Layers of the 3D view: Historical images, Space (a night sky behind the terrain), Peak names and, where the data exists, Bedrock and Super-resolution, described below.

Ice surface of each year

Every year's surface is the ice on the day of that year's satellite picture. It is a physical model fitted to every dated elevation model of the glacier that exists (swisstopo flights since 2017, the 2021 and 2022 lidar, the two swissALTI3D editions), with the GLAMOS mass balance and a fitted rate of ice flow between and beyond them. Where a flight or the lidar was flown, the surface of that day is reproduced; elsewhere it is a model, and the page says so under the picture.

Ten glaciers have a surface for each year from 2016 to 2026. Grosser Aletsch comes first; the nine others are made like it, and I list only what differs.

Grosser Aletsch9

  • Every year is the surface on the day of its satellite picture, fitted to all dated elevation models of the glacier: 13 swisstopo flights (2017 to 2025), the 2021 lidar and the two swissALTI3D editions.
  • Between and beyond the surveys the surface follows the GLAMOS mass balance per elevation band, the melt timing and a fitted rate of ice flow. The tip lake is flat.
  • 2018 to 2025 are measured on most of the strip; 2016 is extrapolated, 2017 is measured on part of the ice, 2026 is a provisional estimate. Older pictures use the surface of 2016.
  • ICESat-2 laser heights check the years that have shots.

A model, not a measurement, except on the days and cells a survey covers. Science mode (open it from the Scientific field notes button at the top of this panel) shows for each year how the surface was found, its uncertainty and its checks. The full account is in the field notes.

Bedrock

Where the terrain file has a bed, the layer Bedrock cuts the strip open as a block diagram: ice above, bedrock below. The bed is swissBEDROCK, a model: under glaciers it is swissALTI3D minus a modelled ice thickness.8

Switching the ice off shows the bed with a rock look painted from the ice-free ground around it, and hollows in the bed get a small lake. The colours and the lakes are modelled, not measured.

Where GLAMOS has radar for the glacier, Thickness cross-section draws a line across the glacier and gives its surface and bed as a chart (downloads as SVG, PNG and CSV).

Super-resolution

An optional layer for Aletsch, Gornergletscher, Fieschergletscher, Rhonegletscher, Unteraargletscher, Corbassière, Zmuttgletscher, Findelgletscher, Feegletscher and Triftgletscher, 2016 to 2026. The Sentinel-2 picture of the year has been run through a super-resolution model (SEN2SR, 10 m to 2.5 m).10

The extra detail is the model's estimate, not something the satellite saw. The layer is off until you switch it on. All other pictures on the site are never sharpened this way.

Lakes

In front of the Rhone, Findel and Zmutt glaciers a lake can have grown over ground that the model holds as a slope. Where the picture of a year shows water (found from its colour), the terrain there is set to the level of the lake that the model already holds, so the water lies flat. A lake without such a level in the model is left as it is.

Old photographs

"Historical images" (3D view, one year) shows historic photographs of the lower tongue for eight glaciers: Aletsch, Gorner, Fiescher, Unteraar, Zmutt, Rhone, Findel and Fee. They come from the ETH Library's Bildarchiv; where the camera stood and looked was worked out by volunteers on Smapshot (HEIG-VD). Click a photograph, or a camera symbol on the terrain (switch it on in the dropdown), and the 3D view flies to that spot and looks the same way, with the same field of view. Drag the line to compare the photograph with the terrain of today.

Only photographs whose date is known to about a year, and where the tongue is in the picture, were kept. Each photograph shows its own licence. A rod on the ice of today marks the estimated height of the ice surface then: from the thinning of the whole glacier in the GLAMOS volume surveys, and before the first survey from the 1850 area with area-volume scaling (Bahr et al. 1997). The range is wide and the figure is an estimate.

Aerial images, trails and panoramas

swisstopo aerial (SWISSIMAGE) can be switched on in the layers menu; it shows only inside the image box of the chosen glacier, in the year you picked. The layer is a series of editions; the year it names is the year the photographs were taken, which can be earlier than the edition (the 2025 edition shows photographs from 2023 at Aletsch). Switch the Sentinel-2 image off to see it alone.

Hiking trails come from swisstopo. The white outline around the trail marks where a person 1.7 m tall can see the glacier tongue from the trail (my own calculation from the elevation model; trees and buildings are not counted). The distances in the hike and bike cards (how far a route passes from the tongue) are computed here too.

Panoramas (Grosser Aletsch only) are the best Street View panoramas of the glacier, from the upper glacier down to the tongue. They open in Google's own viewer; nothing of them is stored here but their ids.17

What to trust

Measured, computed or estimated

Measured

Copied from the source: the Sentinel-2 and Landsat pictures (real satellite images), the outlines of the Swiss Glacier Inventory, the GLAMOS length changes, volume changes between surveys, yearly mass balance of Aletsch, Rhone, Corbassière and Findel, radar ice thickness, ITS_LIVE speeds and, on the Aletsch strip, the surfaces of 2017, 2021 and 2023.

Computed by me

From measured values, and labelled so: sums and averages over your selected years, lengths in 1870 and today, the football pitches, buses and Eiffel Towers, the pin and click values of the ice speed, the distances of the hikes, and the band where the tongue can be seen from a trail.

Modelled or estimated

  • the ice of a glacier in a given year and everything derived from it: the thickness, volume and water tiles of chosen years, the water storage, the Switzerland tab;
  • the ice thickness map of Grab et al. and the bed of swissBEDROCK;
  • the ice surface of the years that are not measured, and the figures of 2026 (the newest autumn has no yearly measurement yet);
  • the rock look and the lakes of the bed;
  • the stripes filled in Landsat 7 pictures (a repair of the picture, not data) and the super-resolved picture.

Where a figure is an estimate the page says so next to it.

Behind the site

About me

I am Jochem Braakhekke, originally from the Netherlands, and the mountains became my home ground. I have worked on glaciers all over the globe and have been a polar expedition guide in both the Arctic and the Antarctic.

I hold a bachelor's degree in Earth Sciences from the University of Amsterdam and a master's degree in Earth Science from ETH Zürich, with glaciology and geology as my main fields. Today I am an academic associate at the remote sensing lab at UZH and a scientific consultant at the Swiss NPOC.

I made Tongues from Space on my own, and I keep it up to date: the pictures, the numbers, the texts and the code.

I believe in pictures: a before-and-after image of a glacier is understood at once and remembered. I hope the site sparks awareness of glaciers and of why they matter to society, for instance because of the water they store.

The code is open on GitHub, where you can also write to me with questions, remarks and bugs. The site is non-commercial; the licences of the code, the texts and the data are listed in the README and under Sources. Follow along on Instagram and TikTok. See also Recogn.ice and For Your Ice.
