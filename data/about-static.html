About Tongues from Space

Using the site

For teachers and journalists

In short. A satellite photographs each glacier every late summer. Flip through the years and watch the tongue pull back.

What you can say

  • The pictures are real Sentinel-2 satellite images: one clear late-summer scene per glacier and year, 2016 to now, with 10 m pixels.
  • Length, area and ice-volume change come from GLAMOS, the Swiss glacier monitoring network. The source sits next to each figure.
  • The ten glaciers are the ten largest in Switzerland by area in the newest inventory.
  • A label says how sure a number is: measured, computed here, or estimated (see How to read this).

Try this in class

  • Open a glacier, press Timelapse. Where does the tongue end each year?
  • Switch to Slider and drag between 2016 and now.
  • Open the Size visualizer. How big is the ice beside your own city?

What it cannot tell you

  • One picture is one clear day, not the whole summer.
  • Ice volumes and the 3D surfaces between surveys are models.
  • The newest figures are provisional.

The details are under Limits and Method.

Why Tongues from Space

A glacier ends in its tongue: the lowest stretch of ice, thin and fast-melting. Change shows here first. Each summer the tip pulls back and leaves bare rock, a lake or a new valley floor. A 10 m satellite pixel is fine enough to see it.

Pictures come first, figures beside them. A before-and-after is understood at once and stays in memory.

Every picture is a real satellite image of the day shown, not a rendering. One optional exception, the Super-resolution layer in 3D, is labelled.

The aim: awareness of glaciers and why they matter, for instance for the water they store. That is why the ice also appears in litres, swimming pools and Lake Zurich. — Jochem Braakhekke

How to read this
  • Colour. Red marks loss. Blue marks ice and water.
  • Time. Years are glaciological years: 1 October to 30 September, named after the year they end in. 2017 is October 2016 to September 2017. A summer picture and the value of its year belong together (see Years and periods).
  • Pictures. One scene per year, the clearest late-summer day, not the whole season. Natural colour is what an eye sees; false colours are a code (see The colours).
  • Charts. Every year has a bar; a red line under the label marks the years you selected. Sums include both ends of your selection.
  • How sure. Measured, computed here, or estimated (see Measured, computed or estimated). The page says so next to an estimate.
  • Gaps. A dash means no figure exists. A small raised number points to its source.

The pictures

The satellite images

Choosing the scene

One real Sentinel-2 scene per glacier and year: the clearest view between 1 July and 20 September. Each acquisition is scored for cloud and snow. The best September scene wins, then August, then July.

Pictures are rendered from Level-1C data with the Copernicus Browser true colour process, which keeps shaded slopes natural.

The picking tool

For some glaciers a separate picking tool chose each year's scene. It scores every acquisition from 1 August to the middle of October for cloud, cloud shadow, mountain shadow, snow and haze, in the bottom 3 km of the tongue and over the whole image. It prefers days near 30 September and keeps one satellite orbit (one fixed flight path) for all years.

  • No cloud, cloud shadow or big mountain shadow in the bottom 3 km (loosened only for a year that would otherwise have no scene).
  • At most 10 % cloud anywhere in the image.

Why not after the 20th

That is the rule for the automatic choice. Swiss glacier monitoring counts in glaciological years (1 October to 30 September), and GLAMOS measures at the end of the melt season. So September images match the annual figures: the picture of summer 2017 shows the ice at the end of the glaciological year 2017 (see Years and periods).

After about 20 September, fresh snow often hides the tongue. In 2024 the limit is 14 September: heavy snow fell on the 15th.

Sentinel-2

Two European satellites of the Copernicus programme (2A since 2015, 2B since 2017), looking straight down on a strip 290 km wide.

Detail (spatial): pixels of 10 m (blue, green, red, near infrared), 20 m (six bands) or 60 m (three). Detail under about 10 m is invisible. Natural and False 1 use 10 m bands; False 2 uses 20 m bands and looks softer; False 3 uses one 20 m band (B11).

Colour bands (spectral): 13, from visible light (about 443 nm) to shortwave infrared (about 2190 nm).

Revisit (temporal): each satellite returns every 10 days; both together every 5 days at the equator, more often at higher latitudes. Over Aletsch: about 3 acquisitions a week, up to 4 (32, 33 and 41 days between 1 July and 20 September in 2023 to 2025). Clouds, shadow and fresh snow remove many of them.

The colours

Each picture combines three bands (slices of the light the satellite records) as red, green and blue. The four buttons in step 2 carry nicknames: Natural, Infra (False 1), Bloody (False 2), Hot? (False 3). False colours are a code, not what an eye would see.

  • Natural (B04, B03, B02): as the eye sees it. Snow white, rock grey, plants green.
  • False 1 (B08, B04, B03), infrared: near infrared shown as red. Plants red, snow white, bare ice grey, water dark or blue. Sets bare ice, snow and vegetation apart.
  • False 2 (B8A, B11, B12): snow and ice red, clouds white, rock pale, water black. Experimental; handy for telling ice from cloud.
  • False 3 (B04, B11, B02, as a ratio picture): ice and snow yellow to orange, rock blue. Red is the red/SWIR ratio, green the blue band, blue the SWIR (shortwave infrared).
Why only these ten glaciers

The Swiss Glacier Inventory SGI2023 lists 1,289 glaciers with a total area of 860 km². The ten largest hold 28 % of it; 1,048 are smaller than 0.5 km² and 673 smaller than 0.1 km².

At 10 m per pixel, a glacier of 0.1 km² is only about 1,000 pixels: its tongue is a few pixels wide, and a retreat of a few metres a year is less than one pixel. The ten largest have tongues many pixels wide that retreat by tens of metres, and that shows.

The numbers

Years and periods

Swiss glacier monitoring counts in glaciological years, 1 October to 30 September. A yearly value is named after the year it ends in: 2017 is October 2016 to September 2017; the year ending in September 2026 is the 2026 value (the 2025/2026 measurement period).

That melt season ends where the summer-2017 satellite picture is taken, so a picture and its year's value belong together. Each chart bar is one such year.

Selecting years

Years you select in step 2 count with both ends. A sum over 2017–2019 adds 2017, 2018 and 2019: the change from autumn 2016 to autumn 2019. One picture of 2017 gives the values of 2017 alone.

Charts show every year and mark the chosen ones with a red line under the label. Sums sit under the chart and in the tiles. Ice volumes (Swiss ice, a glacier's ice) are stocks at the end of a year, read at the end of September.

Estimates

Anything derived from yearly values for a chosen period (thickness, volume and balance of a glacier, the Swiss loss, the melted water) is an estimate from GLAMOS's yearly mass balance, calibrated on measured volume surveys. It is not a new measurement of the period.

Glacier figures

Area

All figures are from GLAMOS; a dash means no value. Area: Swiss Glacier Inventories SGI1850 and SGI2023. When tributaries separate, part of an area drop is a change of definition, not only melt. The card compares 1850, 1973 and the newest inventory.

Length

Length change is measured at the tongue; negative means retreat. GLAMOS publishes only the change between surveys, never the length, so lengths are derived:

  • Latest length: read from a GLAMOS overview figure with one length per glacier, for the year where the glacier's last bar ends (2025 for most; 2023 for Corbassière and Zmutt, surveyed every few years).
  • "Length in 1870" (or the year of the first survey): that length plus all retreat recorded since. Aletsch: 22.11 km + 3.387 km = about 25.5 km.
  • Exact only if the surveys follow each other without gaps and reach the figure's year. Otherwise the tile shows "≈", and the real value may differ by the retreat of the missing years.

The Change tile and the yearly bars

  • Change adds the length surveys that end in the chosen years (the survey starting the autumn before the first chosen year counts, as it ends in that year). It says when surveys cover a shorter period: one survey can span several years, and the newest data end before the newest images.
  • Retreat per year shows every year from 2016 on and marks the chosen ones.
  • Where GLAMOS measured length only over several years (for example 2017 to 2020 at Corbassière), the change is spread evenly and the bars are dashed. Years with no survey stay empty.
Thickness and volume

Volume change from surveys

Mean ice thickness change, volume change and mass balance (ice gained or lost, in metres of water per year) come from this glacier's GLAMOS volume-change survey. Aerial surveys give elevation models of the surface; the difference between two shows the ice lost in between.

  • The newest survey starting closest to 2016 sets the headline loss and period. It is not your selected years.
  • Mass balance: the volume change as metres of water per year, with a density of 850 kg/m³; the ± is GLAMOS's uncertainty.
  • Only surveys covering at least 90 % of the glacier count.

The three tiles follow your selected years. They add up (volume; thickness, the volume over the survey's mean area) or average (balance, as GLAMOS measured it, not scaled) the yearly mass balance of those years, scaled to the glacier's volume survey. So they are estimates. Without yearly values for your years, they show the survey period.

Radar ice thickness

Ground-penetrating-radar measurements along lines on the surface, between 1958 and 2017 depending on the glacier. The map shows the mean of every 50 m cell: the thickness when measured. Today the ice is thinner.

Thickest ice is one number with its year and source: the maximum of the ice thickness map of Grab et al.11 in that map's reference year (2017 for Aletsch). The pin marks its cell. The card gives the map's uncertainty there, towards thicker (+) and thinner (−) ice, as the data collection states it.

A model result, not a measurement: radar lines calibrate an ice-flow model that fills the gaps. Only a radar or seismic maximum measured after the map's reference year would replace it, and the card and pin would say so. The layer "Ice thickness 2016 (modelled)" shows the whole map.

Ice thickness change per year

GLAMOS's yearly mass balance for the image years, as metres of ice (the balance in water divided by 0.85). Aletsch, Rhone, Corbassière and Findel are measured directly each year; the other six show the line of all Swiss glaciers. Switch between Per year and Cumulative (bars added from autumn 2015); your selected years are marked and summed.

A mass balance and a surface-height change are different measurements, but over decades they add up to nearly the same. The headline above the tiles comes from elevation-model surveys, which span several years each.

Ice speed

Where it comes from

The layer Ice speed and the Stats card use the ITS_LIVE annual mosaics of image pair velocities, version 2.1 (120 m, one mosaic per RGI region, 2013 to 2024).4 The product tracks features on the ice surface between pairs of satellite images (Landsat 8/9, Sentinel-2 and Sentinel-1).

Values are copied and only cut to the glacier's picture box. Under 5 m/yr the product cannot tell ice from rock (rock beside the glaciers reads 1 to 2 m/yr): nothing is drawn there, and only ice inside the oldest outline is shown.

On the map

  • The layer shows the picture's year (slider: the right-hand year; timelapse: the frame). Years outside the file have no speed map, and a note says so.
  • A pin marks the year's fastest ice: the highest value along a line from the tongue up the glacier, after a running median (a smoothing that ignores outliers) over about 400 m. Other branches can be faster.
  • Click the glacier with the layer on: the speed there in every year (the mean of the 3 × 3 cells around the click).
  • Pin, click values and card are computed here from the product, and say so. Not shown in 3D or in the downloads.
Ice and water

Ice in Switzerland

Swiss glaciers held about 45.1 km³ of ice at the end of 2025 (GLAMOS, Swiss-wide series, which gives the volume of every year since 1915). The explorer uses the years from 2016. 1 km³ of ice gives about 0.9 trillion litres of water.

Ice of one glacier

Grab et al.11 modelled the ice around 2016 (each glacier has its own reference year in the map, 2017 for Aletsch). From there the explorer uses what GLAMOS measured for this glacier:

  • its volume survey: how much ice one metre of yearly mass balance is worth for it;
  • its yearly balance (its own where measured, else that of all Swiss glaciers): spreads the loss over the years;
  • for the newest autumn, with no yearly balance yet: the Swiss loss of that year, turned into a balance (same ratio of ice volume to balance as in the survey years) and spread the same way.

Big glaciers lose a smaller share than the Swiss average, which small glaciers dominate, so this gives more ice than a plain national percentage. A best estimate, not a measurement.

Water and comparisons

The Switzerland tab turns the ice lost over your chosen years (from the end of the year before the first to the end of the last) into litres and four comparisons: Olympic swimming pools, Lake Zurich, the yearly Swiss tap water, and household water use per resident (9 million residents, 142 litres a day, SVGW). The text chips on the cards use the same sizes:

  • Eiffel Tower: 330 m high (with antennas, toureiffel.paris)
  • Football pitch: 105 × 68 m = 7,140 m² (usual international size)
  • City bus: about 12 m long (standard bus, an articulated one is 18 m)
  • Olympic swimming pool: 50 × 25 × 2 m = 2.5 million litres
  • Lake Zurich: 3.9 km³
  • Swiss water supply: 931 million m³ in 2022 (SVGW)

Ice surface of each year

Each year's surface is the ice on the day of that year's satellite picture. It is a physical model fitted to every dated elevation model of the glacier (swisstopo flights since 2017, the 2021 and 2022 lidar, the two swissALTI3D editions), with the GLAMOS mass balance and a fitted rate of ice flow between and beyond them. Where a flight or the lidar was flown, that day's surface is reproduced. Elsewhere it is a model, and the page says so under the picture.

Ten glaciers have a surface for each year, 2016 to 2026. Grosser Aletsch comes first; the nine others follow the same method, and only the differences are listed.

Grosser Aletsch9

  • Fitted to all dated elevation models of the glacier: 13 swisstopo flights (2017 to 2025), the 2021 lidar and the two swissALTI3D editions.
  • Between and beyond the surveys: the GLAMOS mass balance per elevation band, the melt timing and a fitted rate of ice flow. The tip lake is flat.
  • 2018 to 2025 are measured on most of the strip; 2016 is extrapolated, 2017 is measured on part of the ice, 2026 is a provisional estimate. Older pictures use the surface of 2016.
  • ICESat-2 laser heights check the years that have shots.

Bedrock

Where the terrain file has a bed, the layer Bedrock cuts the strip open like a block diagram: ice above, bedrock below. The bed is swissBEDROCK, a model: under glaciers it is swissALTI3D minus a modelled ice thickness.8

With the ice switched off you see the bed with a rock look painted from the ice-free ground around it; hollows get a small lake. The colours and the lakes are modelled, not measured.

Where GLAMOS has radar for the glacier, Thickness cross-section draws a line across it and charts its surface and bed (downloads as SVG, PNG and CSV).

Super-resolution

Optional, for the ten glaciers, 2016 to 2026: the Sentinel-2 picture of the year run through a super-resolution model (SEN2SR, 10 m to 2.5 m).10

The extra detail is the model's estimate, not something the satellite saw. The layer is off until you switch it on. No other picture on the site is sharpened this way.

Lakes

In front of the Rhone, Findel and Zmutt glaciers a lake can have grown over ground the model holds as a slope. Where a year's picture shows water (found from its colour), the terrain there is set to the level of the lake the model already holds, so the water lies flat. A lake with no such level in the model is left as it is.

Old photographs

"Historical images" (3D view, one year) shows historic photographs of the lower tongue for eight glaciers: Aletsch, Gorner, Fiescher, Unteraar, Zmutt, Rhone, Findel and Fee. Source: the ETH Library's Bildarchiv; volunteers on Smapshot (HEIG-VD) worked out where each camera stood and looked.

Click a photograph, or a camera symbol on the terrain (switch it on in the dropdown): the 3D view flies there, same direction, same field of view. Drag the line to compare with today.

Kept: photographs dated to about a year, with the tongue in the picture; each shows its own licence. A rod on today's ice marks the estimated ice height then: from the whole glacier's thinning in the GLAMOS volume surveys, and before the first survey from the 1850 area with area-volume scaling (Bahr et al. 1997). The range is wide; the figure is an estimate.

Aerial images, trails and panoramas

swisstopo aerial (SWISSIMAGE): switch it on in the layers menu. It shows only inside the image box of the chosen glacier, for the year you picked. The year it names is when the photographs were taken, which can be earlier than the edition (the 2025 edition shows photographs from 2023 at Aletsch). Panoramas (Grosser Aletsch only): the best Street View panoramas of the glacier, from the upper glacier down to the tongue. They open in Google's own viewer; only their ids are stored here.17

What to trust

Method

A picture, in four steps

  1. Every Sentinel-2 pass from 1 July to 20 September is scored for cloud and snow.
  2. The clearest day wins: September first, then August, then July.
  3. The picture is rendered from that day's Level-1C data with the Copernicus Browser true colour process. Pixels are never sharpened, except in the labelled Super-resolution layer.
  4. For some glaciers a picking tool chose the scene with stricter rules and one satellite orbit for all years.

A number

  • Measured figures are copied as the source states them, with the source beside them.
  • What the page adds (sums, averages, comparisons) is computed here, and labelled.
  • Where nobody measured (the ice of a glacier in a given year, a surface between surveys) a model fills in, and the page says estimated.

A surface in 3D

A physical model, fitted to every dated elevation model of the glacier, to the GLAMOS mass balance and to a fitted rate of ice flow. Details: The 3D view and the field notes.

Limits
  • Cloud and snow. A clear day may not exist. Clouds, shadow and fresh snow remove many acquisitions; after about 20 September snow often hides the tongue. The picture is then the best of a poor set.
  • Scene choice. A picture is one day. The day differs between years, so snow and shade differ too. Compare with care.
  • Sharpness. At 10 m per pixel, detail under about 10 m is invisible. A retreat of a few metres a year is under one pixel, which is why only the ten largest glaciers are shown.
  • Measured or modelled. The ice thickness map, the bed, a glacier's ice in a given year, and 3D surfaces between surveys are models. A surface is measured only on the days and cells a survey covers.
  • Timing. Surveys have their own periods, and the newest data end before the newest images. Figures for 2026 are provisional.
Measured, computed or estimated

Measured

Copied from the source: the Sentinel-2 pictures (real satellite images), the outlines of the Swiss Glacier Inventory, the GLAMOS length changes, volume changes between surveys, yearly mass balance of Aletsch, Rhone, Corbassière and Findel, radar ice thickness, ITS_LIVE speeds and, on the Aletsch strip, the surfaces of 2017, 2021 and 2023.

Computed here

From measured values, and labelled so: sums and averages over your selected years, lengths in 1870 and today, the football pitches, buses and Eiffel Towers, the pin and click values of the ice speed, the distances of the hikes, and the band where the tongue can be seen from a trail.

Modelled or estimated

  • the ice of a glacier in a given year and everything derived from it: the thickness, volume and water tiles of chosen years, the water storage, the Switzerland tab;
  • the ice thickness map of Grab et al. and the bed of swissBEDROCK;
  • the ice surface of the years that are not measured, and the figures of 2026 (the newest autumn has no yearly measurement yet);
  • the rock look and the lakes of the bed;
  • the super-resolved picture.

Where a figure is an estimate, the page says so next to it.

About me

I am Jochem Braakhekke, from the Netherlands. The mountains became my home ground. I have worked on glaciers around the globe and guided polar expeditions in both the Arctic and the Antarctic.

I hold a bachelor's degree in Earth Sciences from the University of Amsterdam and a master's degree in Earth Science from ETH Zürich, with glaciology and geology as my main fields. Today I am an academic associate at the remote sensing lab at UZH and a scientific consultant at the Swiss NPOC.

I made Tongues from Space on my own and keep it up to date: the pictures, the numbers, the texts and the code.

I use an AI assistant (Claude) to write code, find and read sources and draft texts. I decide what goes in, and I check what it produces before it is published.

The code is open on GitHub, where you can also write to me with questions, remarks and bugs. The site is non-commercial; the licences of the code, the texts and the data are in the README and under Sources. Follow along on Instagram and TikTok. See also Recogn.ice and For Your Ice.
