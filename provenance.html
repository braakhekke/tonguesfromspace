The satellite pictures are real photographs from space. The 3D ice surface under them is not: for each year it is a reconstruction. For this glacier, these notes show what was measured, what was calculated to fill the gaps, and how far to trust the result. Every number refers to the tongue (chapter 1) and is computed for this site or taken from a named source.

Kinds of surface

  • measured: a dated DEM or lidar surface.
  • interpolated: between two measured surfaces.
  • extrapolated: beyond the last one, at its rate.
  • provisional: 2026.
  • Date: every year is the surface on 30 September of that year (the end of the glaciological year).

A measured surface is moved from its survey day to 30 September by the same model. So a surveyed year shows the survey surface lowered by the melt between the survey day and 30 September, not the raw survey.

Sharing the loss between years

  • Solid: the GLAMOS balance sets how the loss between two measured surfaces falls into each year.
  • Dashed: extrapolated at the last segment's rate (2026 also with its own balance).
  • Not used: measured years.

Uncertainty

Median over the tongue ice, red whisker the 90th percentile. Calibrated on the same ICESat-2 shots. 2026 has none yet: it is provisional and its balance was read from the GLAMOS figure.

Independent checks

  • ICESat-2 also fitted the 2023 anchor day and σ, so it is not independent (rmse is leave-one-granule-out).
  • ASTER and stakes are checks only.

Ice bed: radar points against the bed model

Experimental outlines of the tongue

Yearly lines around the lowest 2 km of the tongue (layer "Experimental outline"). An experiment: not an official outline, not a measurement.

  • Who draws them. A neural network, GlaViTU (Maslov, Persello, Schellenberger and Stein, "Globally scalable glacier mapping by deep learning matches expert delineation accuracy", Nature Communications 16, 43, 2025, doi 10.1038/s41467-024-54956-x), with the weights glavitu_regional_ALP from that study. It reads the six optical Sentinel-2 bands plus height and slope, and gives every 10 m pixel a probability of glacier ice.
  • Which picture. The same Sentinel-2 scene the site shows for that year. The line is where the probability is 0.5, cleaned of small pieces.
  • Smoothing. Each line is resampled every 5 m and smoothed along its length by 20 m, to remove the 10 m pixel steps. Cosmetic.
  • Scenes. …
  • What is shown. Only the lowest 2 km, as an open line around the tip. It fades out towards its loose ends.
  • What can go wrong. One scene is one day: fresh snow or cloud shadow can move the line, so a year can look like an advance. Debris-covered and snow-covered ice are hard for such models. The lines were not checked against a survey, so do not read yearly changes as measurements.
  • Computed here. Areas and distances come from the pixels, not from a source. The reference outline in the viewer is the official inventory outline (SGI 2023).
  • Not settled. The licences of the weights and of the height tiles are still to be read before anything like this is published.

swisstopo special flights on the site

Glacier thickness 2016 (layer)

The layer "Glacier thickness 2016" colours the glacier by how thick its ice is. It is the ice thickness map of Grab et al. (2021), "Ice thickness distribution of all Swiss glaciers based on extended ground-penetrating radar data and glaciological modeling", Journal of Glaciology 67(266), read from the ice thickness layer of swisstopo (GLAMOS data).

  • A model, not a measurement. Only the radar points (next chapter) are measurements.
  • One state for every year. The map shows the glacier around 2016 ("2016" is a round name: the reference year differs by glacier, 2017 for Aletsch). The ice has thinned since, and the layer does not change when you choose another year.
  • Cut to the glacier. The layer follows the glacier outline; nothing is drawn on the rock around it.

Radar points (layer)

The layer "Radar points" shows ice thickness measured by ground-penetrating radar: radar waves sent down into the ice are reflected by the rock beneath, and the time they take gives the thickness. Source: GLAMOS, "Glacier ice thickness from ground-penetrating radar", release 2020 (doi:10.18750/icethickness.2020.r2020, CC BY 4.0). The surveys were made between 1958 and 2017, depending on the glacier.

  • Measured, with a date. These are the only thickness numbers on the site that come from a measurement. A point gives the thickness in the year of the survey, not in the year you are looking at.
  • One dot is one cell. Each dot is the mean of the radar points inside a 50 m cell (computed here). Where a cell was measured in several years, the dot is marked and a click shows all of them.
  • In 3D. Only about ten pins are shown, one on the middle line in each stretch of the glacier, to keep the view fast.
  • Against the model. Where the radar points and the thickness layer disagree, the radar points are the measurement.

Ice speed (layer)

The layer "Ice speed" shows how fast the surface of the ice moves, as a yearly mean. Source: ITS_LIVE annual mosaics of image pair velocities, version 2.1 (120 m, one mosaic per RGI region, 2013 to 2024), NASA MEaSUREs ITS_LIVE project, Gardner et al. (its-live.jpl.nasa.gov, CC BY 4.0). The product tracks how features on the ice surface shift between pairs of satellite images (Landsat 8/9, Sentinel-2 and Sentinel-1).

  • Copied, not made here. The values are the product's, only cut to the picture box. The map shows the year of the picture you are looking at; a year without a map shows nothing.
  • Slow ice is left clear. Below 5 m/yr the product cannot tell ice from rock (rock beside the glaciers reads 1 to 2 m/yr), so nothing is drawn there. Only ice inside the oldest outline is shown.
  • Surface speed. The speed of the surface, not of the deeper ice; a yearly mean says nothing about the seasons.
  • Coarse. At 120 m the mosaic misses narrow ice streams and the last tens of metres of a thin tongue.

Super-resolution layer (3D view)

The optional layer "Super-resolution" in the 3D view shows the Sentinel-2 picture of a year after the super-resolution model SEN2SR (tacofoundation, model card licence cc0-1.0) has been run over it: 10 m pixels become 2.5 m pixels. It exists for the years 2016 to 2026 of the ten Swiss glaciers of this site that have such a picture; other years show the normal picture.

  • An estimate, not a measurement. The extra detail is the model's guess; the satellite did not see it. Do not read crevasses, boulders or the edges of debris off this layer.
  • Off by default. Every other picture on the site is the original Sentinel-2 image, never sharpened, and no number on the site comes from this layer.
  • Credit. Contains modified Copernicus Sentinel data.

Size page, Area (2D): an outline laid on a city

The Size visualizer in Area view lets you drag the outline of this glacier onto a city or onto another glacier. Nothing is drawn or estimated for it beyond what is listed here.

  • Outline and area. The glacier outlines are those of the GLAMOS Swiss Glacier Inventory SGI2023, the latest available (state of 2023, Unteraar and Trift 2024; the glaciers have shrunk since), and SGI1850 for the old extent (CC BY 4.0). The area is the one the inventory states.
  • True size. The outline is only moved, never scaled or turned into another shape: it keeps its true size wherever you drag it.
  • Cities. The outlines are the municipal boundaries (swissBOUNDARIES3D), so they include lake area where the boundary does. A city you type comes from OpenStreetMap (administrative boundary, © OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL, found with Nominatim). The boundary of Zermatt reaches up to the Matterhorn and the glaciers around it.
  • Picture under the outline. In Switzerland the aerial picture is swisstopo SWISSIMAGE, the 2023 edition (© swisstopo); elsewhere and as the background the Sentinel-2 mosaic (cloudless 2024 by EOX, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0).
  • Computed here, not from a source. The span (the distance between the two farthest points of the outline), the area of a typed city and the "fits" figures are calculated from the outlines. A pitch is 105 × 68 m.

Size page, Volume (3D): all the ice as one cube or ball

In Volume view the ice of this glacier becomes one cube or one ball of the same volume, drawn at true size on the terrain next to a city or landmark. It is a size comparison, not a model of the glacier.

  • Volume. Grab & Melchior (2020), Swiss Glacier Thickness, release 2020 (SwissGlacierThickness-R2020), doi:10.3929/ethz-b-000434697, CC BY 4.0: "Ice volume (km3) homogenised for the year 2016", as the file states it. Modelled from radar measurements; the reference year differs by glacier (2015 to 2017).
  • Loss. GLAMOS, Swiss Glacier Volume Change, release 2025 (non-commercial): the change in volume between two surveys, as stored. Total Swiss: GLAMOS, Swisswide Glacier Mass Balance, release 2025, doi:10.18750/massbalance.swisswide.2025.r2025 (non-commercial), for all Swiss glaciers.
  • Computed here. The edge of the cube, or the diameter of the ball, of that volume; the mean loss per year (the volume change divided by the years between the two surveys); the years to melt (the 2016 volume divided by that rate); every ratio. The cube or ball has the volume of the ice, not the shape of the glacier.
  • Debris content. Debris shows in the look of the cube or ball, not in its size: dark grit, silt and small stones are spread through the ice, part of them in a few thin tilted layers like folded moraine bands, the rest scattered. How much follows the share of non-ice pixels inside the glacier's SGI2023 outline on the SWISSIMAGE aerial. The volume and the size of the ice body stay those of the stated ice volume.
    Non-ice pixels in the outline
    The share is computed here by scripts/build_debris_share.py (data/debris-share.json): a pixel counts as debris or rock when it is grey or brown and not bright; ice and snow are bright or tinted blue. It is not a measured debris cover and not a debris volume. SWISSIMAGE is a mosaic of flights of different years, so it is a rough measure. Of the ten glaciers, Unteraar and Zmutt have the highest computed share and carry the most grit, Findel and Rhone the lowest.
  • Physics. The bodies are rigid with assumed friction (0.45) and bounce (0.15); gravity is scaled 60 times so that cubes of this size fall like dice. These are choices for the look, not measurements.
  • Lake floors. Where a lake is shown, the floor is swissBATHY3D, Federal Office of Topography swisstopo (1 m grid averaged here to 20 m). Ice floats with 0.917 of its edge (a ball: 0.823 of its diameter) under water, computed from the densities of ice (917) and water (1000 kg/m³); in a lake shallower than that it rests on the floor.
  • Terrain and buildings. Mapterhorn terrain tiles (swissALTI3D © swisstopo in Switzerland); tall buildings from swissBUILDINGS3D 3.0 Beta, © swisstopo, those whose stated total height (GESAMTHOEHE) is over 25 m.
